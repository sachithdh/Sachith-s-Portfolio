# On Simplicity in Design

Antoine de Saint-Exupéry wrote that *"perfection is achieved not when there is nothing more to add, but when there is nothing left to take away."* In software, we rarely practice this.

## The Additive Bias

We are wired to solve problems by adding. A page feels empty? Add a sidebar. Users aren't engaging? Add a notification badge. The product feels stale? Add a feature.

Each addition seems reasonable in isolation. But compound them over months and years, and you get software that groans under its own weight. Every button, every menu item, every configuration option is a small tax on every user who encounters it.

## What Remains

The products I return to daily share a quality: they feel *inevitable*. There's nothing to remove because every element earns its place. Consider:

- **A text editor** that opens instantly and gets out of your way
- **A terminal** that does exactly what you ask, nothing more
- **A note-taking app** that's just a blank page and a cursor

These tools don't compete for your attention. They serve it.

## The Practice

Simplicity isn't a starting point — it's a destination. You get there through:

1. **Building the complex version first.** You can't simplify what you don't understand.
2. **Watching real people use it.** Your assumptions about what matters are probably wrong.
3. **Cutting ruthlessly.** If a feature serves fewer than 80% of users, it should be hidden or removed.
4. **Sitting with discomfort.** A simple design will feel *too sparse* at first. That's usually a sign you're on the right track.

## The Cost

Simplicity is expensive. It requires more thought, more iteration, and more difficult conversations than complexity does. It's always easier to add than to subtract.

But the result is software that *respects* its users — their time, their attention, their cognitive load. And that, I think, is worth the cost.

> The ability to simplify means to eliminate the unnecessary so that the necessary may speak.
> — *Hans Hofmann*
